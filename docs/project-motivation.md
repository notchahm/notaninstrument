Midi controllers are cool. I don’t know why – maybe it’s because it feels like they can make playing a musical instrument more like playing with a toy. Maybe because they can occupy some interface between digital and analog in an intuitive way. Maybe because midi allows you to orchestrate complex actions with sequencers and effects pedals that you wouldn’t otherwise be able to do without. 

At the same time, it seems like midi controllers are like an instrument without an instrument. I don’t want to have to hook up my controller to a computer before I can start playing it. I want the immediacy of picking up an instrument and playing it. Yes, there are digital pianos and digital drum kits with built-in sounds but I feel like there should be more modularity. If I want to try out a new controller without changing the sounds, that should be easy and not involve plugging into a computer.

After agonizing about this dilemma for a while, I finally settled on getting an AKAI MPK mini play to “play” around with. While the built-in sounds and speaker are not the best, it’s a fun device that’s super portable and lowers the barrier between the occasional spontaneous desire to play some music and actually making that happen. But rather than satisfying my midi desires, it reinforced this nagging thought – why can’t I turn any midi controller into a “play” version by just adding a small module that contains a midi interpreter, builtin sounds, and audio outputs? Why isn’t there already a cheap, mass-produced electronic dongle that already does this that’s readily available for purchase off of a reputable online retailer?

So I became determined to build one myself. I’m a music and engineering nerd with experience programming and a love for building, so it shouldn’t be that hard, right?

First thing I needed was a good dev board with a powerful microcontroller. This device shouldn’t be a fully fledged computer running an operating system and DAW software, though I had considered going down the raspberry-pi route, I wanted something more streamlined and more bespoke. Lower power and instant-on capabilities. But it needed to be more powerful than an Arduino – it should be able to process high fidelity audio signals, mixing and manipulating them in real time, and maybe be able to do some sampling, sequencing, and apply digital effects. 

My first choice was the Teensy 4.0. This is a wonderful dev board that has one of the fastest microprocessors, and conveniently has an audio shield breakout and accompanying audio library that I’ve used for other audio projects. In hindsight, it (or it’s big brother the Teensy 4.1) is probably the most complete starting point for this kind of project, but there are a couple of problems: 1) there is no out-of-the-box solution for receiving midi signals as a USB host, which was the first problem that I was looking to tackle, and 2) It’s one of the more expensive development boards, with the Teensy 4.1 + audio shield + USB host adapter combo.

More economic alternatives that I wanted to consider were the ESP32 and the Raspberry Pi 2350. Although I have had good experiences using various ESP32 development boards for IoT projects, I wanted a board that had Native USB MIDI support, a ARM cortex processor, and strong IO capabilities. I also was interested in exploring a new platform for development – so I started with the Pico 2.

The RP2350 won me over because PIO gets you a software-defined USB host (PIO-USB) without having to gamble on how mature any particular chip’s native USB host stack actually was. That felt like the safer bet at the time, and it paid off: I got MIDI-in working with rppicomidi’s usb_midi_host driver on top of Adafruit TinyUSB Host, and I actually got a note to trigger sound – press a key, hear a short WAV play back, no computer involved. That felt like a real milestone.

And it wasn’t just the software – by that point I had real peripherals wired up and working around it. An SSD1306 OLED over I2C for status output. The PCM5102A DAC (the cheap GY-PCM5102 breakout) feeding a pair of small 2” 3W speakers through a PAM8403 class-D amp, so I was actually hearing it through real speakers instead of headphones. A microSD card holding the sample data – I went through a couple of SPI breakout options (including one of Adafruit’s 3.3V-only boards) before landing on one that got along with the RP2350’s logic levels. I even got 5-pin DIN MIDI working, through a set of Treedix DIN-5 breakout jacks and an Adafruit MIDI FeatherWing, so pre-USB gear had a way in alongside the USB host port. The whole thing was held together with an OTG adapter cable so the Pico 2 could act as USB host and still get power from somewhere else.

It was also held together with duct tape in the code, and I knew it while I was building it. Decay was tied to note-on instead of note-off, so every note played the same pluck no matter how long I held the key. I was calling `sin()` per sample per voice, which is fine for one voice and a real problem the moment there’s more than one. Mixing voices just clipped hard instead of scaling down gracefully. And I was reading each voice’s sample data straight off the SD card inside the mix loop, which is fine for a single stream and falls apart the moment several notes need to play at once without stuttering.

More importantly, I ran into the wall I probably should have seen coming from the start: the RP2350 only has 520KB of SRAM. That’s enough to hold one short stitched-together WAV, but nowhere near enough for what I actually wanted – a real multi-sampled instrument, with real velocity layers, like the free Salamander Grand Piano library. To make that fit I’d either have to shrink the soundbank down to something toy-like, or stream samples off SD in real time per voice – which is exactly the pattern that was already giving me trouble with a single voice, now multiplied by however much polyphony I was hoping for.

So instead of trying to engineer around RP2350’s ceiling, I went looking for a chip with real memory headroom. That’s what led me to the Waveshare ESP32-P4-WIFI6-DEV-KIT: 32MB of PSRAM, enough to hold a properly trimmed-down but still real Salamander soundbank entirely in memory, no SD streaming during playback required. Two RISC-V cores with DSP/FPU extensions, which matters once I’m not just replaying samples but mixing several at once and eventually running real effects on top. And native high-speed USB-OTG host hardware, on paper a more capable path to MIDI input than RP2350’s software PIO-USB trick.

“On paper” ended up doing a lot of work in that sentence. How mature that native USB host support actually is in practice turned out to be the first thing I had to go test, not assume – which is exactly where the current bring-up effort picks up.
